# 2026-09-03 — Phase two, and three wrong answers before a right one

Implemented a structural lineage check. It is the first mechanism in `whence` that can emit
`contradicted`, and the interesting part is not the code — it is that my first two designs were
wrong and only real data said so.

## What it does

Compares a model's transformer body against its declared base's. A fine-tune cannot change
`hidden_size`, layer count, head counts, or intermediate size. If those differ, the declaration
cannot be what it says it is — established from a few hundred bytes rather than gigabytes of
weights.

Agreement establishes nothing further, and the scenario's negative set forbids `verified` for
exactly that reason. Every fine-tune of a base carries that base's body, so a passing check narrows
the field to "one of many". The temptation to overclaim is strongest right where the check has just
succeeded.

## Three field sets

**First attempt: architectures, hidden_size, layers, heads, kv-heads, intermediate, vocab_size.**
Tested against 45 real declared fine-tunes: **9 of 35 flagged**. Every one false. A task head
legitimately changes `architectures` — `gte-reranker-modernbert-base` reports
`ModernBertForSequenceClassification` where its base reports a masked-LM class — and added tokens
change `vocab_size`, which is why four Qwen3 embedding models "mismatched". A 26% false-positive
rate on the exact metadata the tool exists to interpret.

**Second attempt: body fields only.** Down to **1 flagged** — `mistralai/Voxtral-Mini-4B-Realtime`
against `Ministral-3-3B-Base`, differing on `hidden_size`. I nearly shipped it and captured it as
the scenario. Then I looked at the base's config: it has **no top-level `hidden_size` at all**. It
is a composite model that nests its text dimensions under a sub-key, and I was comparing `3072`
against *absent*.

That is absence read as a negative answer — the error this whole project exists to prevent —
committed by the mechanism built to detect it, and it would have accused a real publisher of a
mistaken declaration on the strength of how they format a JSON file.

**Third: body fields, comparing only fields present in both.** Zero flagged, zero false positives.

## What the measurement actually supports

Zero false positives over 22 comparable pairs. **The detection rate is unmeasured** — the sample
contained no mistaken declaration, so nothing exercised the contradiction path in the wild.

So the benchmark scenario is `structural-compatible`, a real true derivation where the check passes
and the verdict stays `unverifiable`. The contradiction path is covered by unit tests with synthetic
configs, and the substitution is stated: benchmark scenarios use real captures, and there was no
real instance to capture. Renaming the scenario from `structural-mismatch` was the honest response
to not finding one.

Of the 45 pairs, 10 had no fetchable config and 13 shared too few comparable fields. The check
reaches a usable comparison for roughly half of declared fine-tunes and returns `unverifiable` for
the rest. A modest instrument, and the bound is in the decision log rather than implied.

## The pattern, again

Every wrong answer here came from reasoning about what a fine-tune *should* preserve, and every
correction came from running it against models people actually published. That is the same lesson as
`whence`'s first resolver run and `deleted-namespace`'s premise, and it keeps arriving in the same
shape: the design was plausible, internally consistent, and wrong in a way only contact with the
world revealed.

Cheap, too. Three rounds of 45 config comparisons cost a few minutes and prevented shipping a check
that would have been wrong a quarter of the time.
